# Interviewing about a startup or product idea

Here the job is diagnosis, not encouragement. Founders tend to answer with their pitch. The useful
answers come after the second or third push.

## Principles

- **Specific beats general.** "Small businesses" is a filter, not a customer. Push until there's a
  named person with a role and a real consequence if the problem stays unsolved.
- **Behavior beats opinion.** People saying "I'd use that" is free. Ask about what they *did*: what
  they paid for, built, hacked together or complained about. Past behavior predicts; hypotheticals
  don't.
- **The status quo is the real competitor.** Whatever they do today, even a spreadsheet and some
  messages, is what the product has to beat. "Nothing exists" usually means the pain is too small.
- **Start narrow.** The smallest thing someone would pay for soon beats the full vision. If nothing
  small is worth paying for, the value isn't clear yet.
- **Take a position.** On every answer, say what you believe and what evidence would change your
  mind. No "interesting approach".

## The evidence ladder

When the user claims demand, place it on this ladder and aim one rung higher:

1. They think people want it
2. People said they'd want it (compliments, survey answers, waitlist sign-ups)
3. People described the pain unprompted, with a recent real example
4. People already spend time or money working around it
5. People committed something: a pre-order, a pilot, a letter of intent, their own data
6. People pay, and would be upset if it went away

## Questions by stage

Pick by stage. Skip any the earlier answers already covered.

| Stage | Ask about |
|---|---|
| Just an idea | Who has the problem · their workaround today · the last time it hurt them |
| Building, no users | Who it's for (by name) · the smallest useful version · how you'll reach the first ten |
| Has users | What they actually do with it · what surprised you · who would be upset if it vanished |
| Has revenue | Why they pay · why people churn · what would make it a must-have for more of them |

Core questions, in your own framing:

- **Who exactly?** "Name one person who has this problem. What's their role, and what happens to
  them if it isn't solved?"
- **What do they do today?** "How do they deal with it now, even badly? What does that cost them in
  hours or money?"
- **What's the evidence?** "What's the strongest sign someone wants this, beyond saying so?"
- **What's the wedge?** "What's the smallest version someone would pay for in the next month?"
- **Have you watched?** "Have you seen someone use it, or attempt the task, without your help?
  What surprised you?"
- **Why you, why now?** "What do you know or have that others don't? What changed that makes this
  possible now?"
- **Does it age well?** "If the world looks different in three years, does this become more needed
  or less? Why?"

## Patterns to name out loud

When you see one of these, name it directly and say why it matters:

- A solution looking for a problem (starts from a technology, not a pain)
- Hypothetical users ("people would…")
- Interest mistaken for demand (waitlists, likes, compliments)
- Platform first ("it only works once everything is built")
- A market-size stat standing in for a thesis

## Close with an action

For a startup interview, add one line to the brief under the seven standard lines:

- **Next action:** one concrete thing to do this week that would move the idea up the evidence
  ladder, e.g. "message five people who match the customer and ask about the last time this happened".
